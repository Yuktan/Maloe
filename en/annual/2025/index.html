In previous annual reviews, I would begin with the three most memorable things I gained. This year, the harvest was sparse; life does not always arrive as abundantly as autumn. Back in October, I worried that I would have nothing to write. In the end, a little quiet helped me gather these scattered thoughts.

I played a game on Instagram: the first four words you spot in a jumble of letters will be your mantras for 2026. So I will begin with those words.

Mantra

Health, Connection, Change, Break.

01

Health

HEALTH

For a woman past thirty who wants to keep growing, health really matters. Changes in hormones can affect energy and well-being more noticeably. I have long relied on ibuprofen for primary menstrual pain. This year, Eimo gave me a thoughtful gift: KOWA's Japanese medicine for period pain. I wanted to mention it to friends who also experience hormone-related stomach cramps before their periods.

More important than medication is staying active and adjusting my diet. Coach Li and I have agreed that next year I will give up coffee and tea, or at least stop drinking coffee on an empty stomach, and work out three or four times a week through gym sessions, cardio, or Pilates.

Looking back on 2025, I often felt unable to do what I wanted: migraines, or an inability to focus deeply. Beyond health, poor sleep was part of it. In November I began a small bedtime ritual: empty my mind and silently give thanks. I think of the driver who got me to the airport, the colleague who exchanged a gift box at the last minute, the sunset, the wind. I cannot say whether it is mystical or simply effective, but I fall asleep faster, sleep more deeply, and have fewer nightmares tangled up with work and the past.

02

Connection

CONNECTION

It has been a year since Cong left. Every so often, like rain arriving without warning in Jakarta, tears suddenly spill over. It is private and difficult to speak of. I have barely dared to mention him this year; I do not really know whom I could tell. Several friends and colleagues have also faced sudden disconnections. When they cry or confide in me, their pain resonates with mine. Last year's thought, “Everything flows; nothing stays,” urged me to learn to live with this. And yet…

Sometimes I underestimate the power of connection between people, especially someone who consistently brought you warmth and encouragement. Even after a long time, you still catch a glimpse of him.

When my assignment in Indonesia ended and I was leaving through Jakarta airport, I suddenly realized that ten years had passed since I first landed there. Some think I stayed with the Indonesian market because I loved the place. Perhaps that is not quite it. A bond simply drew me in: you learn the language, come to understand the culture, meet so many people, and naturally stay. I have often considered giving up and changing direction. Like anyone, I tire of the problems here, the difficult relationships, the limits of the environment. But whenever I waver, I remember what Cong told me when I had just begun working in this market: “You have to be patient enough to see it through.”

Even when I ask too much or lean too heavily on others, this place and its people respond with generosity and lasting support. Cong and every connection I have made here help me feel safe and grounded whenever I return on my own.

Thank you, Cong, for every time you told me, “You're already doing so well,” and for taking care of me in Indonesia for so long. I miss you very much.

I have always hoped that I, too, can be a source of positive energy for others. I used to complain that I was the office trash can: colleagues often came to me to vent. Coach Li would tell me I mistook the feeling of being needed for the safety of belonging. I am gradually realizing that accumulated negativity can wear us both down. If we have a choice, why wait until disappointment piles up before helping each other out? I would rather offer encouragement now. I treasure the colleagues who cheer me on through long working days, saying, “Let's try again,” or “Believe in yourself.” The road is long and difficult. I hope we can walk it together, with a little more hope than disappointment.

I did not do this especially well this year. Next year, I will reconsider how I connect with others and where I need boundaries: how to reach out without being swept into blind competition or becoming a tool without human care.

03

Change

CHANGE

There is not much to say about change. I am tired of merely enduring it. Underneath that feeling is a lack of steadiness within myself. Choosing change means facing vulnerability; following through with resilience is the precious next step.

04

Break

BREAK

When friends saw this word, many looked puzzled. Was I quitting my job? Taking a rest? Break can also be a verb: to break through.

Climbing Mount Rinjani for a second time was one of the wishes I worked hardest to fulfill this year. Before the climb, I wrote a diary entry about 2016, when I had come so close to the summit. I was probably fitter then, but I gave up. The image of the peak never left me.

Eventually I reached the summit and came home safely. Yet this gift to myself in my thirties felt like the steepest part of the climb. Even as my feet kept moving, my mind pulled in opposite directions: “You can stop any time and come back,” and “You're here now; even if the descent takes until night, you have to reach the top.” It felt like daily life: unable to rise, unable to lie back down.

“The summit is a ceremony; the descent is the practice” has stayed pinned to my WeChat chat list. The mountain will always be there. Reaching the top takes a moment; the life waiting after the descent may be no easier than the climb.

So next year, I really do need a break.

Last But Not Least

LAST BUT NOT LEAST

My favorite book this year was Higher Than the Mountain. One passage stayed with me: you do not need a reason to climb.

For centuries, people have asked what climbing mountains means. The act acquired a metaphor of overcoming adversity, and the summit became a tangible goal to strive for.

In the real world, though, a summit is a geological accident: at some point, a pile of rock or a glacier rose higher than its surroundings. Reaching one of the many coordinates on Earth is, in itself, meaningless.

To many, accepting uncertainty and immense fear for that goal seems absurd. Yet walking alone toward an empty mountain, bearing hardship and facing the risk of death for something with no worldly value: this is the dignity of a free climber, and part of the beauty and singularity of being human.

My favorite song was Shan Yichun's version of dear friend, where complicated reality meets the ideals we pursue.

Chasing color, illusion, hope, and disappointment.
Even beauty in this world is complicated.
There is always a gulf between the self you seek and the shape reality takes.
What is love? We both come from broken places,
and spend more time learning how to feel.

My favorite creator this year was Links.

Outdoor photography and trekking can be full of romantic storytelling and heroism. Links folds an honest expression of himself into them with a plain touch. I see distant, almost unreachable scenery, but I can also feel the weight of every step he takes.

He released two videos in December. Several times I thought he would reach the Matterhorn as he had the previous year. But standing in a snow hollow just over sixty meters below Huba's summit, he bravely chose to turn back.

Last year he said: “It took me a long time to reach the Matterhorn, just as it took many years to reach you. Perhaps we all have a Matterhorn of our own. From the moment you see it, the gears of fate begin to turn. But you never know if you can stand on the summit until you start walking, step by step.”

This year he said: “Turning back bravely, I think we have already given it our best. We wanted the summit deeply. As Shi Tiesheng said, if the journey is remarkable, then the journey is the ending. This is far from my ending. As long as we remain curious, we will never stop exploring.”

Every summit story can be read in different ways. It is only one slice of a life. What matters is keeping on.

My favorite live performance was the Cantonese musical The Impossible Trial. A passage from “Scattering White Rice” asks what becomes of attachment at the end.

In a life that passes so quickly, how do we let go of what binds us?
Will pride and shame fade away together?
Do love and hate give rise to each other, or cancel each other out?
When all seems over, let it be like scattering a handful of white rice.

Even a lifetime of resentment may someday seem to have flowed away.
Clouds close in; the bright evening sky sinks lower.
When all seems over, let it be like scattering a handful of white rice.

Film Camera

My favorite purchase this year: the Pentax 17

Last year's mantra was learning to delay gratification. My first film camera really did make me look more carefully at every moment worth pressing the shutter for. Recording and revisiting them brings a particular kind of joy. At the end of the year, I organized a girls-only gift exchange at work and gave someone a disposable film camera. I wrote this wish for her:

This year, the biggest thing I learned is about delayed gratification. We don't always need to feel happy right away, succeed right away, or get everything we want instantly. It's okay to wait.

Just like the film camera you take the shot now, but the magic shows up later. I hope this little camera encourages you to enjoy the moments first and let the good things develop in their own time.
