2023 Annual Review (Early Edition)

An early look back at work, short dramas, brands, and the little things that changed how I see myself.

01

Opening

2024 is almost here. Part of me can’t wait to see where I end up, and part of me can’t believe how quickly time is slipping by. I made some big decisions in the first half of the year, gritting my teeth through them. They changed more than I expected. That’s why I can write this review early, even with nearly 10% of 2023 still left.

02

What I learned: work and trust

First, a piece about organizations: “A Crisis of Trust and the Balance of Responsibility.”

It put a name to why I kept wearing myself down at my old job. Because he trusted me, my manager handed me too much responsibility, including things that should have belonged to him or others. I couldn’t carry it all, fell short, and felt I’d let him down. Then I pulled back from the responsibilities that actually were mine. Our loss of trust fed on itself.

It also answered questions that used to feel like mysteries to me. Why do some very capable people never build lasting trust with their boss, while others who don’t seem exceptional do? Understanding it is only step one, though. You have to act on it to change anything. I missed my chance to build that trust with a manager I respected. All I can do now is remind myself for next time. Oh well.

From there I read “Adults and Organizations,” “JD.com’s Dilemma,” and “Why Bosses Don’t Hear the Truth.” They gave me a lot to think about, though workplace problems need context. I don’t want to copy someone else’s answers wholesale.

03

Short dramas and making things

Second: short dramas and content creation.

This might be the first real year of short dramas going global. Without a business model that works, making them is basically just entertaining ourselves (yes, I’m laughing at myself). Still, I can talk about them as an ordinary viewer instead of someone in the industry. Maybe that’s my way of closing this chapter.

If we call short dramas content creation, they face the same impossible triangle: quality, originality, and volume. On short-video platforms, they rely heavily on recommendations and relatively cheap paid ads to reach people. They usually make money from low-cost per-episode payments or product placements.

To make that work, teams have to keep costs down and raise their odds of a hit. Volume often beats originality and quality on the priority list. That brings a few risks:

  • For creators: formulas are faster than new ideas. Big emotional hooks and easy payoffs have a better shot at keeping people watching than nuance or depth. If everyone keeps competing on speed, many teams may run out of ideas and have to make less—or accept rougher work.
  • For viewers: the more they watch, the pickier they may get. Similar shows will crowd each other out, so the industry will need to reach beyond its usual audience. I’m not sure the current production model can make stories that do that. I’m not optimistic.
  • For the business model: take my opinion with a grain of salt. Some people see short dramas as a way to trade traffic, where making a profit matters more than quality. Some sell that traffic and welcome more competition. Others enter hoping to make something they can call their own. For them, the test is whether they can keep that goal when money pulls the other way.

I am curious about how good and how efficient AI-made short dramas could be. Just curious, though—I’m not waiting to watch them. This reminds me of an answer I read on Zhihu a few years ago (I think it was by Kai-Fu Lee):

Question: If AI could write stories and make cartoons for children, what would that be like? What would you hope for or worry about?

Answer: I don’t have big expectations, but I’d be curious to see what it would be like if it became possible.

AI is already doing useful things in film. This idea may not be around the corner, but one day it could make scripts or videos children love, perhaps more cheaply and even more engagingly.

But we should ask a deeper question. What is a story for children meant to do? Sell more tickets, or help you connect with them? If it’s about connecting, what do you want to say? And why ask a machine to say it?

A good story carries its creator’s intention. It isn’t only there to please. Even an ordinary story can stay with a child if you tell it while they sit on your lap.

Telling stories takes an understanding of people. In the end, you want the human part to shine, don’t you?

04

Things I buy and the brands behind them

Third: consumer products and brands.

(Imagine this in 4566’s voice, though she didn’t say it.) I love spending money. Who doesn’t? Looking at the things I’ve bought over the past few years is surprisingly interesting. Give your shopping habits a little time and they start showing you what you really want underneath. A line from the podcast Feng Tou Quan made something click for me:

In China this year, perfume sales are down overall while home fragrance is growing. Perfume may be more about going out and appealing to others; home fragrance is something you enjoy for yourself. Add the rise of meditation and tarot, and you can see a thread connecting these trends.

Sometimes I only skim an article once and move on. But I keep coming back to pieces about products I like, especially coffee, and finding something new. For now, I’m still looking in from the outside. I need to actually get my hands dirty to understand more.

Here are some titles I kept coming back to:

  • “The Coffee Wars” (2018)
  • “The Coffee Wars 2021”
  • “The Coffee Wars 2023”

Saturnbird Coffee — Wu Jun:

  • “Talking with Saturnbird: How an Experience-First Brand Grows Through Cycles”
  • “Talking with Saturnbird’s Wu Jun About Creating, Inspiration, and Wandering the City”

I’ve been drinking Saturnbird for years, and I book their recycling events whenever I can. I admire how Wu Jun speaks in public: he’s consistent and measured. I often think good creators and good brand builders have a lot in common. There’s plenty to learn from him.

Luckin Coffee:

  • Yang Fei on Luckin’s growth, from Eileen Gu to Coconut Cloud Latte | Centurium Capital
  • Zhou Weiming on how Luckin created a drink selling over 10 million cups a month | Centurium Capital
  • “Luckin Investment Report, Chinese Edition” — Snow Lake Capital

Back in 2019, LiQi and I were walking through Liede in Guangzhou. He spotted a Luckin and asked what I thought of it. As a specialty-coffee snob, I turned up my nose. Looking back, Luckin’s turnaround after 2021 brought “coffee-flavored drinks” to just about everyone in China. It did a lot to make coffee more familiar. What we know is limited by what we’ve experienced so far. I need to leave room for myself—and the world—to change.

05

New habits

  • ChatGPT helped me get through most of the books I used to give up on. Even the dense parts became manageable. Learning how to ask a good question is a skill in itself. One example also made me realize my grasp of the subject was basically at primary-school level. Embarrassing, really.
  • I also talked through how to write better work emails, announcements, reading notes, and even this annual review with ChatGPT.

06

The past eleven months

A quick, one-line look back at each of the past eleven months:

  • January: I went home for the first time in two (or was it three?) years. Lunar New Year visits are like fireworks: bright and over too soon. Going home also makes me more nervous than it used to.
  • February: The amaryllis I’d waited two years for finally bloomed. It reminded me to keep waiting and tending, even when nothing seems to happen. Plants are patient teachers.
  • March: AI could make a convincing version of me from just twenty photos. Suddenly I felt more predictable and easier to reshape, and the idea of being uniquely myself got a little blurry.
  • April: I saw a friend of ten years get married, and thought again about how every reunion also holds another goodbye.
  • May: I headed northwest. Places you’ve dreamed of seeing do eventually appear before you, but they can’t say, “See? I’m just as you remember.” They’ve changed too.
  • June: I wobbled through my first tries on a skateboard. After surfing on land and online, I started wanting to surf the sea too. Even being afraid of the ocean didn’t stop the thought.
  • July: I moved straight into a new, exhausting job. In the tiny gaps when I could breathe, I planted a seed that would help me later: Pilates. By autumn, I was grateful I had.
  • August: Once the new-job glow wore off, the work lost some of its magic. I slowly learned that patience and knowing when to hold back are also ways of knowing myself.
  • September: I let September feel like autumn, not just “Q3.”
  • October: The business I worked on was shut down indefinitely. The world changes in an instant; it can make complete sense and still feel bizarre. That’s what I both love and hate about it.
  • November: I spent a month really sitting with myself. I still don’t know if the questions we talked through that night helped her find a direction.

That’s this year’s review. May the lights keep glowing, the first spark stay alive, and the people we care about stay close.
